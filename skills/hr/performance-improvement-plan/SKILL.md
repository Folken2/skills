# Performance Improvement Plan

## Overview

Turn sustained underperformance into a **documented, fair, and time-boxed process** — not a surprise termination and not an open-ended complaint. The core principle: **a PIP is a good-faith attempt to salvage a capable employee, and the record it produces is what makes the outcome defensible either way.** Every step exists to serve both goals at once.

A PIP has exactly two legitimate outcomes: the employee improves to standard and comes off the plan, or the plan closes with a documented, evidence-backed decision. What it must never be is paper filed to justify a decision already made — courts and arbitrators read the record and can tell the difference.

**Do not run this skill without HR and legal involvement where your jurisdiction requires it.** Employment law, notice requirements, at-will terms, protected leave, and works-council or union obligations vary widely and are not encoded here.

## When to use

- An employee is consistently missing documented expectations and informal feedback/coaching has not worked.
- You need to put a performance concern on the record with specific, measurable standards and a deadline.
- You are running an active PIP — check-ins, evidence capture, and checkpoint decisions.
- You are closing a PIP: confirming sustained improvement, extending, or moving to a documented exit.

## When NOT to use

- The employee is in their first 30–90 days and simply still ramping → use [[hr/employee-onboarding]] (30-60-90 milestones).
- You are running the regular review cycle with an otherwise-accepting employee → use [[hr/employee-engagement]] (performance review cycle and growth plans).
- The issue is misconduct, not performance (policy violation, dishonesty, harassment) → that is a disciplinary process with different standards; involve HR/legal directly.
- You have not yet given specific, documented, timely feedback → do that first. A PIP that lands as the *first* the employee hears of the problem is the single most common reason PIPs fail on appeal.

## Workflow

1. **Confirm the PIP is the right next step.** Verify the issue is performance (not misconduct, not a policy gap, not an unaddressed blocker you caused). Verify prior coaching happened and is documented. If either is missing, fix that first — do not start the clock.
2. **Gather evidence.** Build a factual, dated record: specific incidents, deliverables, metrics, and the standard that applied. Use observable facts, not adjectives. "Missed the March, April, and May sales targets" beats "not proactive". Avoid any characterization that touches a protected characteristic.
3. **Define the standard and the gap.** For each concern, write (a) the role expectation, (b) the observed performance with evidence, and (c) the concrete, measurable standard that must be met. Translate every requirement into something a third party could verify.
4. **Set SMART goals and the timeline.** Each goal is specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound. Set a duration of **at least 30 days; 60–90 days is customary** — long enough to actually improve, short enough to matter. Reserve the right to extend (for example, when the employee takes protected leave) and to close early if performance worsens.
5. **State the support you will provide.** Name the training, coaching, mentoring, tooling, or shadowing the organization will supply, and who owns it. A PIP without resourcing is a trap; document what you committed to.
6. **State the consequences plainly.** Say what happens if the standard is not met by the deadline — up to and including termination or demotion, per policy. Confirm the plan does not alter the employment relationship beyond its terms (for example, at-will status) and note any policy-required language.
7. **Obtain HR/legal review before delivery.** Confirm the document is consistent with how comparable cases were handled, that the timeline and language meet local requirements, and that nothing in it creates unintended commitments.
8. **Hold the launch meeting.** Deliver the plan in person or live (never by email alone). Walk through each concern with its evidence, the standard, the support, the check-in schedule, and the consequences. Invite the employee's perspective and record it — their input can correct factual errors and often reveals a blocker you can fix. Ask the employee to sign to acknowledge receipt; if they decline, note that the plan stands regardless.
9. **Run scheduled check-ins.** Weekly or biweekly, with the manager, plus HR at the checkpoints. Give a **written summary to the employee after each meeting**: progress against each goal, evidence, and what needs to change. Do not skip a check-in — missed check-ins are the fastest way to make the plan look like a paper trail.
10. **Decide at each checkpoint.** At each checkpoint, mark each goal met / on track / at risk / not met against its evidence. Adjust support and clarity as needed; do not move the goalposts on a met goal.
11. **Close the plan.** At the deadline, make and document one of three decisions: **sustained improvement** → close the PIP, confirm the standard now holds, and return to the normal lifecycle via [[hr/employee-engagement]]; **partial/insufficient progress with genuine trajectory** → extend once, in writing, with the reason and new deadline; **standard not met** → close with a documented outcome and hand the record to HR/legal for the next step. Never leave a PIP silently expired.
12. **Preserve the record.** Keep the plan, evidence, check-in summaries, acknowledgments (or refusals), and the outcome decision together in the employee file per your retention policy. Purge on schedule; do not let expired plans linger in active records.

## Red Flags / Common Mistakes

| Red flag | Why it's a problem | Do instead |
|---|---|---|
| PIP is the first specific feedback the employee has received | Reads as pretext; fails on appeal; unfair | Give documented, timely feedback and coaching first |
| Vague standards ("raise the bar", "be more proactive") | Unmeetable, unverifiable, indefensible | Measurable, observable standards tied to role expectations |
| No evidence — only adjectives | Cannot be defended or fairly assessed | Dated incidents, deliverables, and metrics |
| Timeline under 30 days, or open-ended | Too short to improve; too vague to enforce | 30 days minimum, 60–90 customary, with a fixed deadline |
| Consequences left unstated | Employee can claim they were never warned | State outcomes explicitly, up to termination/demotion per policy |
| Support promised but not delivered | Makes the plan a trap; destroys credibility | Name the support and its owner; deliver it and log it |
| Check-ins skipped or undocumented | Looks like papering the file | Weekly/biweekly check-ins, each with a written summary |
| Goalposts moved after a goal was met | Bad faith; poisons the record | Adjust only for genuine scope change, documented in advance |
| PIP run without HR/legal review | Legal and consistency exposure | Review before delivery and at every outcome decision |
| PIP left to silently expire | Ambiguity, and no closure for anyone | Explicit close, extend, or exit decision, documented |
| Consequences applied for protected activity | Retaliation exposure | Judge the plan on performance evidence only; involve legal immediately |
| Same plan text reused verbatim across employees | Ignores role- and case-specific facts | Tailor goals and evidence per person |

## Exit criteria

- [ ] The issue is confirmed as performance, and prior documented feedback/coaching exists.
- [ ] A dated evidence record exists for each concern (specific incidents, deliverables, or metrics).
- [ ] Each concern states the role expectation, the observed gap with evidence, and the measurable standard required.
- [ ] Goals are SMART and time-bound; the PIP duration is ≥30 days with a fixed deadline.
- [ ] The support to be provided is named, with an owner, and is actually delivered.
- [ ] Consequences of not meeting the standard are stated explicitly, per policy.
- [ ] HR/legal review completed before delivery; treatment is consistent with comparable cases.
- [ ] The launch meeting was held live; the employee's response was captured and acknowledged (signed or refusal noted).
- [ ] Check-ins ran on schedule with a written summary to the employee after each one.
- [ ] Each checkpoint recorded per-goal status against evidence (met / on track / at risk / not met).
- [ ] A final decision is documented: sustained improvement and closure, one written extension with reason, or a documented exit handoff.
- [ ] The complete record is filed per retention policy, with a scheduled purge date.

## Tools

- `scripts/pip-tracker.py` — turn a PIP's goals, checkpoints, and evidence into a consistent per-goal status and an overall decision signal, so the close/extend/exit call is made on the record rather than on memory. Emit a template with `python scripts/pip-tracker.py --sample > pip.json`, then run `python scripts/pip-tracker.py pip.json`. It also warns on structural gaps that undermine defensibility (no evidence, no check-in logged, deadline under 30 days, missing consequence statement). It is a documentation aid, not a decision-maker: HR/legal own the outcome.

## Sources

Aligned with **SHRM**'s eight elements of an effective PIP (specific incidents, instructions for improvement, a reasonable timeframe of at least 30 days, notice of consequences, reserved right to extend, signed acknowledgment, resistance to blocking conduct, and regular documented check-ins), the **U.S. Chamber of Commerce** guidance on PIP structure (expectations, action plan, next steps if unmet) and its emphasis on objective evidence, and standard HR practice on defensible documentation and good-faith process. Jurisdiction-specific requirements — notice, at-will terms, protected leave and activity, works councils, and union agreements — are not covered here; confirm with HR and employment counsel before delivery.
