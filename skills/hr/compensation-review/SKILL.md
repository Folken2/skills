# Compensation Review

## Overview

Run a compensation review as a **governed, budgeted, and auditable cycle** — not a scramble of manager asks in the last week of the quarter. The core principle: **pay decisions must be defensible on three axes at once — internal equity, external competitiveness, and affordability.** A cycle that satisfies only one of the three creates either attrition, overspend, or a pay-equity problem that surfaces later as a compliance issue.

A full cycle typically runs **12–14 weeks end to end**. Rushing the planning and calibration phases is what produces chaos in the final weeks, not the volume of decisions.

**Compensation is jurisdiction- and policy-sensitive.** Pay transparency and equity-reporting rules, works-council and union consultation requirements, and notice obligations vary widely and are not encoded here. Confirm with HR leadership, Finance, and employment counsel before launching.

## When to use

- You are planning or running an annual or mid-year compensation/merit review cycle.
- You need to build or apply a merit matrix and set the increase budget envelope.
- You are preparing calibration sessions for pay recommendations.
- You are running a pay-equity audit or preparing compensation communications.
- You are handling market adjustments, promotions, or off-cycle equity fixes.

## When NOT to use

- You are running the performance rating cycle itself (criteria, ratings, review conversations) → use [[hr/employee-engagement]]; comp review *consumes* calibrated ratings, it does not produce them.
- You are onboarding or offboarding a single employee and setting final pay or PTO payout → use [[hr/employee-onboarding]] or [[hr/employee-offboarding]].
- You are processing the payroll run after the increases are approved → use [[hr/payroll-processor]].
- You are making an individual counter-offer to a resignation → that is a retention decision; involve HR leadership and treat it outside the cycle.

## Workflow

1. **Align on purpose and philosophy first.** Before any number exists, get written stakeholder agreement on what the cycle must achieve (drive performance, improve engagement, fix equity, retain critical skills), how market-competitive the company intends to be (target percentile), whether performance feeds pay, how promotions are handled, and how much manager discretion is allowed. Misalignment here is the primary source of a derailed cycle.
2. **Agree the budget envelope with Finance.** Set the total pool as a percentage of payroll (commonly 3–5%), and split it into named buckets: **market adjustments**, **merit increases** (usually the largest), **promotional increases**, and **equity fixes**. Model low/medium/high scenarios with the assumptions behind each, and get executive sign-off in writing before opening the cycle.
3. **Refresh market data and pay ranges.** Benchmark each role against reliable survey data for industry, company size, and geography. Re-check job descriptions and levels so you are comparing equivalent work (job evaluation) — roles drift, and a stale level invalidates the benchmark.
4. **Consolidate internal data.** Pull current salary, job level, department, tenure, location, and the calibrated performance rating for each eligible employee. Compute the **compa-ratio** (salary ÷ range midpoint) for everyone — it is the input that stops equally-rated people from getting wildly different outcomes.
5. **Publish the merit matrix and guidelines.** Define the increase for each performance-rating × compa-ratio cell so that higher performers and those below range midpoint receive proportionally more, and those above midpoint receive less (or a lump sum instead of base). Require managers to document any recommendation that deviates from the matrix.
6. **Train managers, then open the cycle.** Give managers a short enablement pack: the matrix, the budget context, compa-ratio explained, guidance on how to explain a number, and the exception path. Managers recommend; they do not decide alone.
7. **Validate recommendations as they arrive.** Watch for the standard red flags in real time: every recommendation clustering on the same percentage, a manager recommending the same raise regardless of rating, inverse relationships (a lower performer receiving a larger increase than a higher performer), and any recommendation creating a new range outlier. Fix these before calibration, not during it.
8. **Run calibration.** Bring managers in the same job family or level together to compare recommendations against evidence and the matrix. Target roughly **three minutes per employee** by having each manager present the rating, the proposed increase, and the evidence. Calibration's job is to test consistency — it must not rewrite an outcome without evidence.
9. **Run the pay-equity audit.** Analyze the *proposed* outcomes by gender, ethnicity, tenure, job level, and department, adjusting for legitimate factors (level, location, performance). Where an unexplained gap appears, correct it in this cycle and record the reason. Do this before approvals so corrections are funded, not discovered later.
10. **Obtain approvals and record them.** Route final adjustments to Finance and leadership per the approval matrix. Log who approved what, when, and on what basis — approval tracking is what makes the cycle auditable.
11. **Communicate, then implement.** Give employees the timeline, the methodology, the market and budget context, and their effective date and payroll date. Managers hold the individual conversations; enable two-way dialogue (all-hands plus a drop-in session) since pay is inherently personal. Then hand the approved adjustments to payroll with the effective date and confirm the run landed correctly.
12. **Close the loop with post-cycle analysis.** Report average increase by department, rating, level, and tenure; actual spend versus the planned pool; how many recommendations needed calibration adjustment; and the final equity-audit result. Survey managers and employees on clarity and fairness, then feed the findings into next cycle's planning.

## Red Flags / Common Mistakes

| Red flag | Why it's a problem | Do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Cycle starts with spreadsheets, not alignment | Rework, disputes, and a derailed process | Get written stakeholder agreement on purpose and philosophy first |
| No written budget envelope before manager input | Uncontrolled spend; rework after approvals | Agree the pool and buckets with Finance up front |
| Performance ratings not calibrated before comp | Pay inherits rater bias directly | Calibrate ratings first, then apply the matrix |
| Managers guessing percentages | Inconsistent, indefensible outcomes | Publish the merit matrix; require documented exceptions |
| Every recommendation clustering on one number | Budget bypass; no differentiation | Flag clustering live and resolve it in calibration |
| Lower performer receives a larger increase than a higher performer | Signals pay is unrelated to performance; equity risk | Validate for inverse relationships before calibration |
| Calibration rewrites ratings without evidence | Bias replaces criteria | Require evidence for every adjustment; document the reason |
| Pay-equity audit run after approvals | Corrections arrive unfunded or too late | Audit proposed outcomes before approvals |
| Stale job levels or benchmarks | Comparing non-equivalent work | Re-run job evaluation and refresh market data each cycle |
| Promotions folded silently into the merit pool | Distorts merit and compresses the promotion budget | Track promotional increases as their own bucket |
| No approval record | Not auditable; disputes are unwinnable | Log approver, timestamp, and basis for each adjustment |
| Increases approved but payroll effective date missed | Trust damage; corrections and retro-pay churn | Confirm the effective date with payroll and verify the run |
| Nobody explains the number | Employees read silence as unfairness | Manager conversations plus transparent methodology and context |

## Exit criteria

- [ ] Written stakeholder agreement exists on cycle purpose, philosophy, target market percentile, and manager discretion.
- [ ] Budget envelope approved in writing by Finance and leadership, split into named buckets, with scenarios modelled.
- [ ] Market benchmarks refreshed and pay ranges (with midpoints) confirmed for every role in scope.
- [ ] Job levels and descriptions re-validated so comparisons are like-for-like.
- [ ] Internal data consolidated with compa-ratio computed for every eligible employee.
- [ ] Merit matrix published and manager enablement delivered before the cycle opened.
- [ ] Recommendations validated for clustering, uniform raises, inverse relationships, and new outliers.
- [ ] Calibration sessions held per job family/level, with evidence required for every adjustment and reasons documented.
- [ ] Pay-equity audit completed on *proposed* outcomes; unexplained gaps corrected and the reason recorded.
- [ ] All adjustments approved per the approval matrix, with approver, timestamp, and basis logged.
- [ ] Communications sent with methodology, context, effective date, and payroll date; manager conversations held.
- [ ] Payroll processed on the correct effective date, and the run verified against approved adjustments.
- [ ] Post-cycle report delivered (spend vs. pool, distribution by rating/level/department, calibration adjustment count, equity result) and fed into next cycle's planning.
