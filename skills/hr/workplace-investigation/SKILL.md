# Workplace Investigation

## Overview

An investigation is a **fact-finding** process, not a decision-making one. The deliverable is a defensible record of what happened — established by evidence, gathered fairly, and written down — not a verdict reached quickly. Most investigations that fail legally fail on process, not on the conclusion: the scope drifted, the notes are missing, the respondent was never told what they were accused of, or the reporter was punished two months later.

Two principles carry the whole workflow. **Due diligence** — you investigate thoroughly enough that a third party reviewing the file would reach the same finding. **Due process** — every party gets equal time and a real opportunity to tell their side, including the respondent, who must always be told the allegation and given a chance to answer.

Three rules that are easy to get wrong:

- **Never promise absolute confidentiality.** Promise confidentiality *to the extent possible* and share on a need-to-know basis. An unkeepable promise destroys the reporter's trust and the file's credibility.
- **Never conclude before the evidence is in.** Interim measures are not findings, and a respondent is not guilty because a complaint was filed.
- **Never investigate alone when the allegation is serious.** Threats of violence, safeguarding concerns, senior/executive respondents, and anything with criminal exposure go to legal or an external investigator before you take a statement.

This skill ends at **findings plus a recommended corrective action**. Executing a separation is [[employee-offboarding]]. A performance shortfall with no misconduct element is a coaching and goals problem — run it through [[employee-engagement]]. An access or data-integrity concern found along the way is a security matter, not an HR one.

## When to use

- A complaint, grievance, or allegation of misconduct has been raised — by an employee, a manager, a customer, or anonymously.
- You need to decide whether a report even warrants a formal investigation.
- You are running the investigation: scoping, preserving evidence, planning interviews, writing the report.
- You are determining findings and recommending corrective action.
- You are auditing a closed case (missing artifacts, unmonitored retaliation, inconsistent outcomes).

## When NOT to use

- **Pure performance shortfall, no policy violation** → coaching, goals, and feedback via [[employee-engagement]]. Opening an investigation into poor output is a misclassification that poisons the relationship and the record.
- **A disagreement about how work should be done** → manager coaching, not an investigation.
- **A low-level interpersonal friction report that both parties want mediated** → informal resolution, documented, with the option to escalate later. Not every conflict is a case.
- **The allegation is serious, criminal, or involves an executive** → escalate to legal/external investigator *before* taking statements. This skill's workflow still applies, but the investigator is not you.
- **You are the subject, a witness, or personally involved** → hand off. Investigate nothing you are a party to.
- **The person is leaving anyway** → the departure still needs a clean close-out via [[employee-offboarding]]; do not let a departure silently extinguish a live allegation.

## Workflow

### Phase 1 — Intake and triage (day 0–2)

1. **Log the complaint verbatim.** Capture the reporter's own words, the date and channel received, whether it was anonymous, the alleged conduct, who is involved, when it happened, and what outcome they are asking for. Do not summarize into a conclusion — "conflict" and "retaliation" are different allegations. Assign a case ID.
2. **Triage severity and immediate risk.** Ask: is anyone unsafe right now? Is the alleged conduct ongoing? Is the respondent a manager of the reporter, or a senior leader? Is there a safeguarding, discrimination, harassment, or criminal dimension? Anything in that set triggers immediate escalation to legal/HR leadership. Triage decides *who investigates*, not *what happened*.
3. **Assess interim measures.** Where there is ongoing harm or an unreviewable reporting line, put interim measures in place: schedule separation, temporary reporting-line change, access restriction, or paid administrative leave. State in writing that interim measures are precautionary and are not a finding, set a review date, and document why the measure is proportionate. Interim measures must never look like punishment.
4. **Conflict-check the investigator.** The investigator must have no reporting line to the respondent, no prior involvement in the events, no personal stake, and no history with either party. Record the check. If it fails, assign someone else — a tainted investigator invalidates the whole process.
5. **Acknowledge receipt to the reporter.** Confirm the report was received, explain the next steps and the expected timeline, state the no-retaliation commitment, and explain confidentiality *to the extent possible*. Never promise a particular outcome, and never promise secrecy you cannot keep.

### Phase 2 — Scope, plan, and preservation (day 2–5)

6. **Write the scope down.** Enumerate each allegation separately, name the policies potentially implicated, define the period under review, and state explicitly what is out of scope. Scope discipline is what stops an investigation becoming an unbounded fishing expedition — and scope creep is what makes cases expensive and unfair.
7. **Build the investigation plan.** For each allegation: the evidence to obtain, the witnesses to interview, the interview order, and target dates. The plan is a living document — update it as facts emerge and keep the versions.
8. **Preserve evidence immediately.** Issue a preservation/litigation-hold notice where appropriate, suspend auto-deletion, export messages, email, calendar, access logs, and badge records to an access-controlled folder. Evidence preservation happens in the first days or it does not happen at all.
9. **Notify the respondent.** Tell them a complaint has been made, describe the allegation in enough detail to allow a real response, give them the relevant policy, explain the process and timeline, and remind them of confidentiality and the no-retaliation policy. Offer support resources and any representation right your policy or local law provides.
10. **Reinforce confidentiality and non-retaliation with everyone involved** — reporter, respondent, witnesses. Remind them that the duty to cooperate is a policy obligation. Put it in writing.

### Phase 3 — Evidence and interviews (day 5–25)

11. **Do the records-based fact-gathering first.** Review email, messages, calendar, access and badge logs, performance records, prior complaints, and CCTV where applicable — then build a chronology before interviewing anyone. Interviewing first anchors you to whoever spoke first.
12. **Interview the complainant.** Take a written, signed statement and collect any documents they hold. Use open-ended questions; never lead the witness toward a conclusion; do not offer opinions about the outcome. Ask what they want to happen, and record it as a request, not a decision.
13. **Interview the witnesses.** Before each interview, review the documents and prepare an outline. At the start, explain the process, the confidentiality limits, the anti-retaliation policy, and the duty to cooperate. Ask open-ended questions that invite narrative, avoid yes/no and leading questions, and offer no conclusions. Take clear notes: who was present, date, time, and who is speaking.
14. **Interview the respondent last.** Put each specific allegation to them, allow a full and uninterrupted response, and invite them to produce any evidence or witnesses of their own. Record their account in their words.
15. **Re-interview where new facts emerged.** Returning to the complainant — and to any witness whose account is now in question — after the respondent has spoken is standard practice, not a sign of a weak case. It is how you test the accounts against each other.
16. **Assess credibility explicitly.** Weigh consistency across multiple tellings, corroboration by independent evidence, internal plausibility, motive, and demeanor — then state the basis for the assessment in the report. "I believed them" is not a credibility finding; a documented comparison is.
17. **Close the evidence record.** Log every item with its source, date received, and custodian, and list the gaps — evidence you asked for and did not get, witnesses who declined or were unavailable. Documented gaps are defensible; hidden ones are not.

### Phase 4 — Findings and outcome (day 25–35)

18. **Determine findings allegation by allegation.** For each: substantiated, unsubstantiated, inconclusive, or not a policy violation. Attach the specific evidence each finding rests on. Do not issue a single global verdict — different allegations in one complaint routinely have different outcomes.
19. **Write the investigation report.** Include the scope, the process followed, the evidence reviewed, the interviews conducted, the chronology, the credibility assessment, the finding per allegation, the policies implicated, and an explicit statement of the investigation's limitations. Keep it factual and free of opinion about the person.
20. **Recommend corrective action; do not decide it alone.** Recommend action proportionate to the finding and consistent with how comparable cases were handled. Route the recommendation to the designated decision-maker (with legal involved where the finding is serious). Consistency across cases is what makes outcomes defensible.
21. **Deliver the outcomes.** Tell the reporter the process has concluded and what will change for them — without disclosing another employee's discipline. Notify the respondent in writing of the decision and the action. Apply the corrective action, and record the date it took effect.
22. **Close the case file.** Archive the report, statements, evidence index, notifications, and decision under the retention policy and any legal hold. Record the case metrics (category, duration, finding, action) for trend analysis.

### Phase 5 — Aftercare (30–90 days)

23. **Check in with the reporter around day 30, and again at 60–90.** Ask directly whether anything has changed for the worse since the report. **Retaliation is the single most common post-investigation claim**, and it is usually invisible in the case file — it shows up in a reassignment, a withdrawn project, a shifted shift pattern, or a dropped rating. Watch for it deliberately.
24. **Feed systemic findings back into the business.** A pattern of substantiated cases points at a policy gap, a training need, or a manager capability problem. Route those to leadership and into [[employee-engagement]] and [[employee-onboarding]] rather than closing the case as an individual incident.
25. **Debrief the process itself.** Did triage pick the right severity? Did the scope hold? Was the timeline met? Record the lessons so the next case is faster and more consistent.

## Red Flags / Common Mistakes

| Red flag | Why it's a problem | Do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Investigating a serious allegation yourself because you are "close to it" | Conflict of interest invalidates the process and the outcome | Conflict-check first; escalate to legal/external investigator |
| Absolute confidentiality promised to the reporter | Unkeepable; destroys trust and credibility when it breaks | Promise confidentiality to the extent possible; need-to-know only |
| Interviewing before collecting records | Anchors the case to the first account heard | Build the chronology from records, then interview |
| Respondent never told the specific allegation | No meaningful chance to respond — a due-process failure | Notify the respondent with enough detail to answer |
| Interim measures look punitive | Converts a precaution into an unrecorded sanction | State in writing that they are precautionary, not findings; set a review date |
| No written scope | Scope creep; unfocused, expensive, unfair investigations | Enumerate allegations, policies, and period in writing before starting |
| One global verdict for a multi-allegation complaint | Hides the findings that matter; invites challenge | Findings per allegation, each tied to evidence |
| "I believed the complainant" as the credibility finding | Not a documented basis; indefensible on review | State the consistency, corroboration, and plausibility analysis |
| Witness interviews with leading or yes/no questions | Produces the answer you fed, not the facts | Open-ended questions; no opinions or conclusions offered |
| No retaliation check after close | The most common post-investigation legal claim | Reporter check-in at 30 and 60–90 days; look for subtle changes |
| Evidence auto-deletion or mailbox expiry during the case | Facts destroyed by default retention settings | Preservation notice and export in the first days |
| Corrective action inconsistent with comparable past cases | Inequality is the easiest thing for a claimant to prove | Compare to precedent; involve legal and the decision-maker |

## Exit criteria

- [ ] Complaint logged verbatim with date, channel, reporter status (named/anonymous), and case ID assigned.
- [ ] Severity triage recorded; escalation triggered where violence, safeguarding, discrimination, criminal exposure, or a senior respondent was present.
- [ ] Investigator conflict-check recorded; no reporting line to, or prior involvement with, the respondent.
- [ ] Reporter acknowledged in writing with next steps, timeline, no-retaliation commitment, and confidentiality limits stated (no absolute promise).
- [ ] Written scope enumerating each allegation, the policies implicated, the period covered, and the exclusions.
- [ ] Investigation plan with evidence list, witness list, interview order, and target dates; updates versioned.
- [ ] Evidence preserved and exported to an access-controlled location; preservation/litigation hold issued where applicable.
- [ ] Respondent notified in writing with sufficient detail to respond, the relevant policy, and process expectations.
- [ ] Chronology of events built from records before the first interview.
- [ ] Complainant interviewed with a signed written statement; witnesses interviewed with documented notes (attendees, date, time, speaker).
- [ ] Respondent interviewed last and given a full opportunity to respond and to produce evidence.
- [ ] Re-interviews conducted wherever new facts emerged; credibility assessed and the basis stated explicitly.
- [ ] Evidence index complete with source, date, and custodian; gaps documented rather than omitted.
- [ ] Finding recorded per allegation (substantiated / unsubstantiated / inconclusive / not a policy violation), each tied to specific evidence.
- [ ] Investigation report written: scope, process, evidence, interviews, chronology, credibility, findings, policies, limitations.
- [ ] Corrective action recommended to the designated decision-maker, proportionate and consistent with precedent; legal involved where serious.
- [ ] Outcomes delivered: reporter informed of process conclusion without third-party discipline disclosed; respondent notified in writing; action applied and dated.
- [ ] Case file archived per retention policy and legal hold; case metrics recorded.
- [ ] Reporter check-in completed at ~30 days and 60–90 days with an explicit retaliation review.
- [ ] Systemic findings routed to leadership and to [[employee-engagement]] / [[employee-onboarding]] where a policy or training gap exists.
